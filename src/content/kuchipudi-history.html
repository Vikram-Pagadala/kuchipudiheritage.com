Before the village: dance in early Andhra

Dance has deep roots in the Telugu country. The tradition handed down in Kuchipudi reaches back to the Satavahana kings, who were known for their love of the arts and who supported dancers dedicated to the deity Andhra Vishnu. Empires rose and fell, Buddhism flourished across the region, and each age left its mark on the dance of Andhra.

By the medieval period the region had a living tradition of dance-drama called Yakshagana, in which a troupe sang, spoke and danced stories from the epics and the Puranas. Kuchipudi grew from this soil.

Siddhendra Yogi and Bhama Kalapam

[[zoom:painting|Painting of Siddhendra Yogi dancing|frame-4x3 gilt|Siddhendra Yogi, honoured as the father of Kuchipudi.]]

The Kuchipudi we know begins with Siddhendra Yogi, a Vaishnava poet and visionary who is traditionally placed in the 17th century. He was steeped in the Yakshagana tradition and was guided by his guru, Narayana Tirtha, the author of the Sri Krishna Leela Tarangini.

The story is told that Krishna appeared to Siddhendra Yogi in a dream and asked him to compose a dance-drama on the parijata flower that was brought from heaven for Satyabhama, his best-loved queen. The result was Bhama Kalapam, which is still the centrepiece of the Kuchipudi repertoire.

Siddhendra Yogi's achievement was a fusion. He drew on the Yakshagana folk tradition and joined it with classical technique, and so made a style that is stately and sensuous, learned and earthy at once. For this he is honoured as the father of Kuchipudi.

Kuchelapuram becomes Kuchipudi: a village of performers

To perform his work, Siddhendra Yogi gathered a group of dedicated Brahmin boys and trained them himself. Facing resistance from the society around them, they settled at Kuchelapuram, the place later called Kuchipudi, and turned a stretch of wasteland into a home for their art.

Tradition holds that in the late 1600s the ruler of Golconda, Abul Hasan Tana Shah, saw the troupe perform and granted the village to the families who practised the art, recording the gift on a copper plate. The families promised in return to keep the tradition alive.

They kept their word. The same family names recur through three centuries of Kuchipudi: Vedantam, Vempati, Chinta, Pasumarthy, Bhagavatula, Mahankali, Tadepalli, Yeleswarapu. Their descendants still live in the village and still hold to the discipline and devotion of the founders.

Three centuries of dance-drama

For most of its history Kuchipudi was a travelling theatre. A troupe, called a melam, was made up entirely of men. Boys began with child roles such as Prahlada, Lava and Kusa. The most gifted went on to play the heroines, and the art of stri vesham, playing women's roles, became one of the glories of the form. A Sutradhara narrated and conducted the performance.

The followers of Siddhendra Yogi wrote more plays, and the repertoire grew to include Golla Kalapam, Prahlada Charitra, Usha Parinayam, Sasirekha Parinayam, Rama Natakam and Harischandra.

The best known troupe of the modern period was the Venkatarama Natya Mandali, founded by Chinta Venkataramayya (1860–1949), which toured dance-dramas across the region until 1936. Vempati Venkatanarayana (1871–1935) performed Bhama Kalapam more than a thousand times and was called Abhinava Satyabhama.

From dance-drama to the solo stage

Solo dancing had always appeared inside the dance-dramas, as an interlude or an ornament. In the early twentieth century it became a form in its own right.

The change is credited to Vedantam Lakshminarayana Sastry (1886–1956). He choreographed solo pieces to ashtapadis, padams and javalis, shaped them into a recital, and taught women as well as men. Tadepalli Perayya (1886–1942) carried Kuchipudi training beyond the hereditary families.

From this point Kuchipudi has lived in two forms side by side: the traditional dance-drama, and the solo recital that ends with the Tarangam on the brass plate.

Recognition as a classical dance

Kuchipudi is centuries old, but its formal recognition is recent. The revival of the mid-twentieth century met real obstacles: there were debates about whether Kuchipudi was classical at all, and misconceptions about its nature.

Gurus and scholars answered them. Pioneers such as Vedantam Lakshminarayana Sastry played a crucial part in dispelling the myths and establishing Kuchipudi as a respected classical form. In 1957 Siddhendra Kalakshetram was founded in the village, with Vedantam Parvateesam among its founding members, to train new generations. Kuchipudi took its place among the classical dances of India recognised by the Sangeet Natak Akademi.

Madras, cinema and the world

In the 1940s a group of young dancers left the village for Madras. Vempati Peda Satyam founded the Kuchipudi Kala Vignana Kendram there in 1943. Vedantam Raghavayya became the first Kuchipudi artist to work professionally in film, and Pasumarthi Krishna Murthy directed dance for nearly two hundred films.

In 1963 Vempati Chinna Satyam founded the Kuchipudi Art Academy in Madras. He refined the technique, created a new body of dance-dramas and trained dancers who carried Kuchipudi to every continent.

The village troupes travelled too. Vedantam Satyanarayana Sarma took the dance-drama tradition to the Festival of India in Paris in 1985 and in the USA in 1986.

Women in Kuchipudi

Kuchipudi was once closed to women. In the twentieth century they stepped into it and changed it. Their presence has added to the delicacy and grace of the dance and has opened the way to a more inclusive future.

Uma Rama Rao was among the first women to win prominence as a Kuchipudi guru. Sobha Naidu became one of the best-known soloists of her time and led the Kuchipudi Art Academy in Hyderabad from 1980.

1974: a flag for the art

In 1974 Vedantam Parvateesam gave Kuchipudi a flag, the patakam: a light pink banner on a sugarcane pole, painted with the plait of Satyabhama. Fifty years later, in December 2024, Kuchipudi Heritage Arts Society marked its golden jubilee in the village with the three-day Kuchipudi Pathaka Swarnotsavalu. Read what the Kuchipudi flag means.

Kuchipudi today

Kuchipudi is now taught and performed around the world. It adapts to the stages and audiences of the present while holding to its classical core, and it speaks across borders in a language of movement that needs no translation.

The task of this generation is to keep both halves of the inheritance: the village tradition of the dance-drama, with its Sutradhara and its Yakshaganas, and the solo form that the world knows. That is the work of Kuchipudi Heritage Arts Society, from the village where it all began.
